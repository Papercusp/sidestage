Three ways to rebuild the Lineup

The Lineup is the one screen a seller operates while they are live on camera. Today it is a five-column spreadsheet where every row holds five open forms and every button looks the same. These are three different answers to that — not three skins. The palette, type and chrome below are the shipped Ticket theme, unchanged; what changes is the information architecture.

What's actually wrong today

Eight faults, all read off the source and a live screenshot of the page. Every direction below is judged on which of these it removes — that's the scorecard, not taste.

1
The grid cannot fit its own panel

Five columns whose declared minimums sum to about 1080px — the commerce cell alone sets min-width: 350px — inside a dock panel that gets roughly 700px.

Observed live: "Product" header clipped to "Pl"
2
Every row is five open forms at once

Five inputs and five buttons per item, permanently armed, on every row. Editing density crowds out the scanning the seller actually needs mid-show.

3
No action hierarchy

Push — the highest-stakes live action on the page — renders identically to Stock, Markdown and Send. All six are .button.tertiary, .66rem type, 1.9rem tall.

4
Targets sized for a spreadsheet, not a live console

30px buttons, a 30px-wide quantity input, 10px badge and label text — operated one-handed while talking to camera.

5
Targeted offers ask you to type a buyer ID

A raw free-text field, inline on every row. A real value on the live site right now is buyer-AL;KJGL;DSKGHSDHGSDFH. Nobody types that.

Observed live in the auction bid list
6
Markdown is a blind percentage

You type a number and commit; you never see the resulting price first. The policy already carries priceFloorCentsByProduct and maxMarkdownPercent — the guardrail exists in the data and is invisible in the UI. A fat-fingered markdown is money.

7
A "lineup" with no order

Nothing on the grid expresses sequence. The run-of-show planner is a separate panel stacked underneath — two disconnected surfaces over the same ordered products.

8
A permanent banner that never says anything

"Guarded seller actions are live" holds prime space above the work with copy that never changes and never needs a response.

The three directions

Each screen below is drawn at 720px — the width the panel actually gets — with the same event and the same four products, so you are comparing architecture and not content.

Direction A

Stage Deck

Stop drawing a table. The show has exactly three states — what's on stage, what's next, and what's waiting — so draw those. The live item gets a card with room for real controls; the queue gets one big obvious verb each; everything else moves into an inspector you open on purpose.

The detail worth stealing regardless of direction

The markdown control shows the resulting price before you commit, and draws the policy's real price floor and max-markdown as a hard stop on the track. Both numbers already exist in SellerEventPolicy.

Removes

  • 1Nothing needs 1080px — cards reflow at any width.
  • 2Rows carry values; forms live in the inspector.
  • 3Push is the only red button on screen.
  • 4Full-size targets, no 30px anything.
  • 5Buyer picker with real people in the room.
  • 6Markdown previews the price and shows the floor.
  • 8Banner replaced by an inline guardrail verdict.

Costs

  • One click to edit. Markdown and stock move behind the inspector — worse for bulk pre-show setup.
  • More vertical space per item; a 20-item lineup scrolls a lot.
  • Sequence is implied by the queue, but there's still no time budget — fault 7 only half-solved.

Best when

  • The seller is live and improvising, deciding what to push next from what the room is doing.
  • Lineups are short — under a dozen items.
  • You want the smallest change that fixes the most faults.

Direction B

Console

Keep the table — a table is genuinely the fastest way to scan twenty products — but take the forms out of it. Rows show values and one verb. Everything else happens in a command bar that acts on the selected row, driven by keys, so an experienced seller never leaves the keyboard.

Why the command bar and not a modal

A modal steals the screen mid-broadcast. The bar is always there, always shows what it will act on, and closes back to the table in one key — so the seller never loses sight of the lineup while editing it.

Removes

  • 1Five value columns fit 700px comfortably.
  • 2Zero forms in the table; one drawer at a time.
  • 3One red key, and it's Push.
  • 4Command-bar targets are full size.
  • 5Offer opens a buyer picker, not a text field.
  • 6Same guarded markdown, with the floor inline.
  • 8Banner gone; guardrails shown where they bind.

Costs

  • A selection model to learn. "Which row am I acting on" is now state the seller must hold.
  • Keyboard shortcuts are invisible to a first-time seller without onboarding.
  • Sequence still isn't expressed — fault 7 untouched, planner stays a separate panel.
  • Touch users get the least out of it.

Best when

  • Lineups are long — twenty, fifty items — and scanning speed dominates.
  • The seller is a repeat operator who runs shows weekly.
  • You want to keep the existing grid component and change what's inside it.

Direction C

Run of Show

The word "lineup" is already a promise of sequence — so make the lineup be the run of show. One ordered timeline with a minutes budget per slot and a clock running against it, and the separate planner panel underneath disappears into it. Commerce controls hang off each slot.

What the clock buys you

"3 min behind plan" is the single most useful number a live seller can be told, and the data to compute it — planned seconds per entry — is already stored by the run-of-show planner. Today nothing displays it during the show.

Removes

  • 1One column of slots; no horizontal pressure at all.
  • 2Controls collapse into the slot you expanded.
  • 3Push to stage is the only red button.
  • 4Full-size targets throughout.
  • 5Offer tab opens the buyer picker.
  • 6Guarded markdown with floor and preview.
  • 7Sequence, timing and pace become the spine.
  • 8Banner replaced by live guardrail feedback.

Costs

  • The largest change by far — it absorbs RunOfShowPlannerPanel and needs its save path, not just a new grid.
  • Tallest rows of the three; a long lineup is a long scroll.
  • Sellers who don't plan timings pay for a column they leave empty.
  • Drag reorder needs a keyboard equivalent to stay accessible.

Best when

  • Shows are rehearsed and the plan is real, not decorative.
  • You want one destination for prep and for going live.
  • Pacing a show is a problem sellers actually report.

If you want my read

A, then C. Stage Deck clears seven of the eight faults for the least structural risk and needs no new data — it is a rewrite of one component. Run of Show is the better product and clears all eight, but it absorbs a second panel and its save path, so it is a plan rather than a change. Console is the right answer only if long lineups and repeat operators are the real workload; it leaves the sequence problem entirely alone.

Three things are worth building whichever direction you pick, because they are fixes rather than choices:

  1. The guarded markdown control — resulting price before commit, floor and max drawn from the policy that already exists.
  2. A buyer picker in place of the free-text buyer ID field.
  3. One primary action per item, so Push never again looks like Stock.

Screens use the shipped Ticket theme tokens verbatim — #FFF8EF ground, #D62B1F brand red, #FFC400 taxi yellow, Inter — so nothing here proposes a re-skin. Products, prices and the buyer handle are drawn from real data on the live event. Nothing on this page is wired to the API.
